An end-of-life alarm is useful when it leaves enough notice for the replacement it schedules. This article calibrated that property directly. For a threshold trigger the notice is monotone in the warning level, every calibration unit has a single critical level, and the conformal quantile of those levels gives a warning level whose probability of failure before the planned replacement is at most $\alpha$ for a new exchangeable unit. The guarantee extends to calibration data censored in any way, at a price that depends on how early units were censored, to cross-fitted calibration on all training units, and, in a long-run sense, to populations that change. A decision-aware choice keeps it, and the number of calibration units sets a floor, $1/(n+1)$, on the failure probability any such choice can certify.

On seven run-to-failure datasets and with four different learners the guarantee held where it should and failed where it should not be expected to hold. Per-cycle conformal lower bounds on the same signal failed on up to 70\,\% of units at nominal 10\,\%, so interval coverage should not be read as alarm reliability. Calibration histories produced by an earlier alarm were as good as complete data. With cross-fitting, certification cost about as much as the best uncertified tuning rules built on the same models. A calibrated level did not survive a change of battery batch; ten pilot cells of the new batch restored it, and so did an online update from the batch's own outcomes, without any pilot failure. The practical recommendation is to state the failure probability an alarm is meant to achieve, to calibrate it on units of the population in which it will run, using replaced units where they were replaced late, and to report how many units the claim rests on. Open problems are a per-unit guarantee under a changing population without pilot failures, online updating with long feedback delays, and validation on field fleets with genuinely censored lives.
