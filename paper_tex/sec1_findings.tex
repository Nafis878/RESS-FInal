The main findings are five. (i) Where calibration and new units were exchangeable, the guarantee held on all seven datasets: pooled failure rates were 3.8, 9.3 and 19.4\,\% at nominal 5, 10 and 20\,\%, whereas a per-cycle conformal lower bound on the same signal and trigger failed on 18--70\,\% of units at nominal 10\,\%. Both held with three further learners, including a neural network on raw sensor windows, and the guarantee held for each of 27 settings of the alarm. (ii) Censoring by an earlier alarm cost nothing: with half of the calibration units replaced before failure, the certified level was identical to the one computed from complete data. Censoring early in life kept the guarantee but made it uninformative. (iii) The certificate's cost premium is governed by the number of calibration units. With a 60/40 split it was large on small datasets; cross-fitting on all training units put the certified alarm on a par with the best uncertified rules built on the same models, never significantly dearer and sometimes cheaper. (iv) The guarantee did not transfer across battery batches (61--74\,\% failures on two held-out batches). Ten pilot cells of the new batch run to failure restored it, and so did online calibration from the new batch's own outcomes without any pilot failure (13\,\% failures over the whole sequence, 10\,\% in its second half). (v) In the decision setting of Kamariotis et al.\ the certified alarm was statistically indistinguishable from their optimised probability-threshold policy on four of five datasets and better on one. Negative results, including three failed hypotheses, are reported with the same weight as positive ones.
